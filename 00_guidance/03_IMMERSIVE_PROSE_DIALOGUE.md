## Purpose

The target is:

**specific person + specific plaCE + immediate pressure + physical action + incomplete knowledge + changing information + consequenCE**

# 1. SCENE ENGINE

Before drafting each substantial sCEne, identify four things:

1. **Immediate objective** — what someone is trying to accomplish now.
2. **Pressure** — what makes that objective difficult.
3. **Changed information** — what the character learns, notiCEs, realizes, or discovers.
4. **SCEne consequenCE** — what is different when the sCEne ends.

Pressure may be ordinary:

- weather;
- distanCE;
- fatigue;
- damaged tools;
- shortage;
- social hierarchy;
- another person's refusal;
- incomplete information;
- obligation;
- time;
- reputation;
- physical danger.

A sCEne does not require a dramatic confrontation. It does require movement in the situation.

If nothing changes, compress the material.

### ReferenCE calibration

The referenCE novel repeatedly gains sCEne energy from:

**ordinary activity → new information → changed interpretation → changed action → consequenCE**

Use the mechanism, not the referenCE novel's fantasy mechanics, modern phrasing, or character voiCEs.

# 2. LIVED MOMENT

Write important events as continuous experienCE.

Prefer:

**perCEption → physical response → action → interaction → interpretation → consequenCE**

over:

**fact → explanation → conclusion**

Anchor the reader in the physical spaCE:

- where people are;
- what they are handling or doing;
- what can be heard, seen, touched, smelled, or felt;
- what movement is possible;
- what object, terrain, weather, or material condition matters.

Use only details that affect perCEption, behavior, atmosphere, or understanding.

Do not inventory the senses.

### Physical causality

Physical details should normally matter.

A horse's condition can change travel.
A damaged tool can change work.
A muddy road can change timing.
A wound can change movement.
A grain shortage can change a negotiation.

Do not insert physical detail solely as decoration.


### Universal sCEne test

Apply these questions to every sCEne, adapting them to its purpose rather than forcing every sCEne to use the same structure:

1. **PlaCE:** Can the reader understand the relevant positions, distanCEs, objects, boundaries, and movement options?
2. **PresenCE:** What are the people physically doing while the sCEne unfolds, and why does that behavior fit the moment?
3. **Causality:** What prompts each consequential action, and how does it produCE the next result?
4. **PerCEption:** What can the viewpoint character actually see, hear, remember, infer, misunderstand, or not know yet?
5. **Interaction:** How do words and actions change the other person's response, options, or social position?
6. **Time:** Which moments need to unfold step by step, and which routine or repeated actions can be compressed?
7. **ConsequenCE:** What changes in knowledge, risk, resourCEs, trust, obligation, status, relationships, or available choiCEs?

These are diagnostic questions, not seven mandatory beats, paragraphs, or descriptive requirements. A quiet sCEne may rely on perCEption and interaction; a physical sCEne may rely on spatial clarity and causality. Do not forCE irrelevant elements into a sCEne.

### Concrete-action rule

When a consequential event matters to the chapter, dramatize enough of its essential steps for the reader to understand how it happens. Do not replaCE the event with a summary of its result.

- Combat: establish the relevant positions, movement, contact or failed contact, response, and immediate consequenCE.
- Investigation: show the evidenCE, observation, interpretation, verification or unCErtainty, and resulting decision.
- Negotiation: show the offer or demand, response, pressure, and any change in terms or position.
- Work and daily life: show the task, relevant constraint, response, and effect on time, labor, resourCEs, or people.
- Relationship sCEnes: show the interaction and observable response through which familiarity, trust, attraction, discomfort, conflict, or distanCE changes.

This is not a demand to dramatize every action. Expand the steps neCEssary to make the consequential change legible; compress routine or immaterial activity. Do not invent missing physical, historical, or character facts to fill a gap. If an essential detail is not established, consult canon/research or flag the unCErtainty.

# 3. INFORMATION AND COMPETENCE

Do not explain competenCE before demonstrating it.

Show a capable character by what they:

- notiCE;
- compare;
- diagnose;
- reject;
- choose;
- misjudge;
- correct;
- or act upon.

When new information arrives, let it affect the sCEne promptly.

**New information → altered perCEption/posture/speech/action**

Do not postpone the consequenCE merely to create a later exposition paragraph.

### Staged revelation

For important discoveries:

**encounter → reaction → interpretation → implication → action**

When a consequential inferenCE affects a decision, keep the distinction between observed evidenCE, inferred explanation, and unresolved unCErtainty clear enough for the reader to follow. Let the character act on the inferenCE without treating it as confirmed fact when the evidenCE does not justify CErtainty.

Do not explain implications the character has not yet understood.

Do not repeat information onCE the reader already understands it unless the repetition changes the character's understanding or decision.

### Historical information

Prefer historical texture through:

- work;
- objects;
- transactions;
- institutions;
- social behavior;
- conversation;
- mistakes;
- consequenCEs.

Direct exposition remains allowed when it is the clearest method. Keep it attached to the current viewpoint and situation.

# 4. CHARACTER PRESSURE

Character traits matter when they alter behavior.

For a recurring character, ask:

- What does this person want now?
- What do they know and not know?
- What are they unwilling to say?
- What habit, strength, weakness, loyalty, fear, pride, or obligation can affect the choiCE?
- What changes because of that choiCE?

Do not forCE a character flaw into every sCEne.

A negative trait is useful when it creates a believable decision, mistake, escalation, hesitation, or consequenCE.

Likewise, grief, fear, loyalty, embarrassment, or pressure should alter behavior rather than exist only as an emotional explanation.

### Knowledge boundary

Characters may act only on information available to them.

Do not give supporting characters convenient knowledge simply because the author needs the sCEne to work.

# 5. DIALOGUE

Dialogue is action between people, not a transcript of information.

Before important dialogue, establish:

- each speaker's immediate objective;
- starting social position;
- relevant knowledge boundary;
- what each speaker wants the other to acCEpt, do, reveal, or stop doing.

Then allow the conversation to move through:

**claim → response → adjustment**

Useful patterns include:

- test → answer → counter-frame;
- report → diagnosis → order;
- objection → rebuttal → changed option;
- personal pressure → revealed weakness;
- warning → resistanCE → conCEssion;
- long explanation → changed decision.

These are patterns, not templates.

Evaluate a consequential response by what it does to the exchange: it may change a position, test a claim, expose a constraint, alter an available option, or deliberately preserve resistanCE. Agreement and escalation are not required; a refusal or evasion must not be forCEd into a change it does not produCE.

### Dialogue rules

- Let turn length vary naturally.
- Do not forCE every line to contain subtext.
- Do not make restrained characters chatty.
- Do not make practical characters philosophical without cause.
- Do not lengthen dialogue merely to create "immersion."
- Do not repeat facts both speakers already know.
- Do not turn ordinary conversation into an interrogation.
- Use silenCE, interruption, hesitation, humor, or physical action when the character's situation calls for it.
- Let the physical environment affect the conversation when appropriate.

A long speech must carry a burden: diagnose, warn, persuade, defend, negotiate, mourn, justify, or decide.

# 6. STATUS, RELATIONSHIP, AND SOCIAL MOVEMENT

In socially unequal sCEnes, track who currently has the ability to:

- demand;
- refuse;
- question;
- threaten;
- grant;
- shame;
- leave;
- or delay.

A conversation should not treat all speakers as socially interchangeable.

Status movement can be subtle. It may occur because someone produCEs evidenCE, demonstrates competenCE, refuses a demand, offers a conCEssion, loses faCE, reCEives help, or becomes dependent on another person.

Do not announCE the status change afterward. Let the reader observe it in changed behavior.

Relationship consequenCEs should also propagate naturally:

**event → changed behavior → changed relationship/options**

When a consequential relationship change matters to a later interaction, carry it forward through relevant behavior or choiCEs rather than restating it as an explanation. Do not forCE a visible follow-up when the relationship has not materially changed or the later sCEne does not call for it.

Do not turn every shared sCEne into a relationship update.

# 7. SCENE RHYTHM AND PROSE

Use varied sentenCE and paragraph lengths according to the sCEne.

Short paragraphs are for genuine emphasis, reversal, shock, humor, or tension.

Longer paragraphs are the default when continuous action, spatial movement, observation, or reflection belongs together. A normal narrative paragraph should carry a complete beat or connected sequenCE rather than one isolated sentenCE.

Dialogue may stand in its own paragraph when the speaker changes. Narrative prose should not imitate dialogue formatting: do not plaCE every action, observation, reaction, or explanatory sentenCE in its own paragraph.

Avoid mechanical patterns such as:

- one-sentenCE narrative paragraph chains;
- action → blank line → action;
- observation → blank line → observation;
- observation → explanation → observation → explanation;
- action → explanation → action;
- "He looked / He notiCEd / He thought / He realized";
- statement → confirmation → statement.

Do not mechanically merge paragraphs to satisfy a numerical target.

When a short narrative sentenCE belongs to the same physical or causal beat as the surrounding prose, keep it in that paragraph. Use a paragraph break only when the reader should feel a genuine shift in action, focus, speaker, time, or emphasis.

The test is continuity of experienCE, not visual fragmentation.

### Summary control

Summarize:

- routine work;
- uneventful travel;
- repeated established activity;
- ordinary time passage.

Dramatize:

- consequential decisions;
- discoveries;
- conflict;
- important social encounters;
- material changes;
- meaningful emotional changes;
- danger.

Spend narrative time where the situation actually changes.

# 8. ACTIVE RESTRAINT

Not acting can be an action.

A character may deliberately:

- wait;
- retreat;
- stay silent;
- conCEde;
- avoid pursuit;
- withhold information;
- let another person speak first;
- abandon an advantage.

When such restraint matters, show what it changes:

**restraint → altered distanCE/information/status/risk → consequenCE**

Do not automatically treat retreat, silenCE, or conCEssion as passivity.

# 11. STORIES GO GENRE AND SOCIAL CALIBRATION

Immersion must remain compatible with the project's established genre.

- **Comedy:** use Mingke's personality, dry humor, social situations, misunderstandings, and character-specific banter. Do not make him an emotionless interrogator.
- **RomanCE:** develop through interaction, trust, attraction, shared experienCE, conflict, and consequenCE. Do not forCE romanCE into sCEnes that do not support it.
- **Martial arts:** show ability through movement, training, weapons, observation, and action when relevant. Do not replaCE historical ability limits with referenCE-novel power scaling.
- **Social protocol:** forms of address, introductions, hierarchy, household roles, age, familiarity, and obligation should affect behavior. Do not forCE modern-style introductions or modern social assumptions.
- **Historical-fiction boundary:** documented history remains factual authority; invented conversations, private interactions, and unsupported local details are fictionalized rather than presented as verified history.
- **Supporting characters:** create them when sCEne logic, social realism, conflict, humor, or continuity requires them. Do not add characters merely to increase cast size or manufacture dialogue.

These are calibration constraints, not separate immersion gates.

- it reads as a sequenCE of lived sCEnes rather than a report;
- important sCEnes have objective, pressure, changed information, and consequenCE;

### Passage-level audit standard

An immersion finding must identify the actual passage or sCEne, state precisely what the reader cannot picture, follow, perCEive, or feel, and recommend a correction that preserves canon. Prefer actionable findings such as “the reader cannot tell where the second attacker is when Mingke turns” over vague notes such as “make this more immersive.”

Audit for:
- spatial or object-handling ambiguity that affects understanding;
- consequential actions skipped between setup and outcome;
- unexplained causal transitions;
- viewpoint knowledge that arrives too early;
- dialogue that exchanges information without interaction or change;
- consequenCEs that are announCEd but not experienCEd;
- paragraph fragmentation that breaks a continuous narrative beat;
- repetitive or decorative detail that adds length without improving experienCE.

Do not mark every item as mandatory in every sCEne. Record only material failures. Do not impose a word-count quota or fix prose through mechanical paragraph merging. A chapter passes when its relevant sCEne-specific requirements are clear and the reader can follow the consequential changes.
